# Improve — is it working, and when to stop

_Reading the evidence yourself, rather than the summary someone prepared for you._

Every system will be reported to you as working. The question is whether you can check.

## What to read

Four surfaces, none of which require technical skill to interpret once you know what they are for.

- **The audit trail** — what the system decided, on what basis, and who approved it. This is the record that matters when someone asks how a particular outcome happened.
- **Confidence distribution** — not the average, the shape. A pile-up just above your threshold is a warning that the number is doing less work than you think.
- **Review outcomes** — how often the human disagreed, and whether the disagreements cluster. Clustering means a fixable systematic problem.
- **Evaluation results** — whether the behaviour you specified in stage three still holds after a change. Without these you are relying on the absence of complaints.

## Corrections become checks

This is the loop that separates systems that improve from systems that merely persist. Each correction is a labelled example of what the right answer was. Captured deliberately, it becomes a test that prevents the same error recurring. Left uncaptured, the same error recurs indefinitely and everyone becomes accustomed to it.

## Knowing when to stop

The hardest and most credible judgement in the whole framework. The signals are usually visible well before anyone acts on them: review load that never falls, an error rate that plateaus above what the process can absorb, cost drift that outruns the benefit, or a workaround culture where people quietly stop trusting the output and check everything anyway.

Withdrawing something that is not working is not a failure of the programme. It is the programme working. An organisation that cannot stop cannot safely start.
