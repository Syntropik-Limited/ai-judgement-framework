# Spot — where AI belongs

_Before asking what AI can do, map what the work actually is._

Most AI initiatives begin with a capability looking for a home: someone has seen a demonstration and asks where it could be applied. That order is backwards, and it is why so many pilots land on work that was never a good candidate.

Start with the work. In any function there is recurring activity that already has a shape: it arrives in volume, it follows implicit rules, someone checks it, and the checking is where the expertise lives. That shape is what makes automation possible or impossible — not the sophistication of the model.

## What to write down

Three things, for each recurring process:

- **Volume.** How often, and how variable. Weekly counts beat impressions; a process that feels constant but runs forty times a month is a different proposition from one that runs four thousand.
- **Who checks it today.** Not who does it — who catches it when it is wrong. That person holds the definition of correct, and you will need them in stage three.
- **What happens when it is wrong.** A rework loop, an unhappy customer, or a regulatory finding. These are not the same and should not be treated the same.

## The trap

The most seductive candidate is usually the one that demonstrates best: visible, familiar, and easy to show working on a handful of examples. It is often the worst place to start, because the qualities that make it demonstrable — small volume, high visibility, unforgiving stakes — are exactly the qualities that make failure expensive.

The candidates worth pursuing tend to be duller: high volume, moderate stakes, rules that are real but tacit, and a checking step that someone quietly does well.
