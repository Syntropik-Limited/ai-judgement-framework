# Spot — where AI belongs

_Before asking what AI can do, map what the work actually is._

Most AI initiatives begin with a capability looking for a home: someone has seen a demonstration and asks where it could be applied. That order is backwards, and it is why so many pilots land on work that was never a good candidate.

Start with the work. In any function there is recurring activity that already has a shape: it arrives in volume, it follows implicit rules, someone checks it, and the checking is where the expertise lives. That shape is what makes automation possible or impossible — not the sophistication of the model.

## What to write down

Three things, for each recurring process:

- **Volume.** How often, and how variable. Weekly counts beat impressions; a process that feels constant but runs forty times a month is a different proposition from one that runs four thousand.
- **Who checks it today.** Not who does it — who catches it when it is wrong. That person holds the definition of correct, and you will need them in stage three.
- **What happens when it is wrong.** A rework loop, an unhappy customer, or a regulatory finding. These are not the same and should not be treated the same.

Naming the checker matters for a second reason. Work of this kind does not remove that person — it changes what they spend attention on, from the routine pass to the exceptions, the standard and the corrections, which is the part only they could do. Say that early and say it plainly. The people who hold the definition of correct are the people the programme depends on, and they will form a view about what is being done to them long before anyone gets round to briefing them.

## Whether the work exists as data

A process can be an excellent candidate and still be impossible to automate, because the work is not reachable. This is not a assessment — it is a precondition, and it usually belongs to IT rather than to you. But Spot is where you find out, and finding out late is what turns a six-week pilot into a six-month one.

Three questions settle it. Does the work exist as data at all — paper and scans cannot be searched, counted or matched until something has turned them into readable records. Can the system reach it in practice and with permission, rather than in principle. And is what comes back good enough to work with.

Expect the answer to the third to be uneven, and plan for the uneven part rather than the average. On real engagements the failures cluster at the awkward edge of ingestion — the document too long to process in one pass, the scan with no extractable text, the malformed file that stalls and quietly retries at your expense — not on the happy path. A candidate whose data is 95% clean is still a good candidate. What matters is whether you know which 5% is not, and what the system does when it meets it.

## The trap

The most seductive candidate is usually the one that demonstrates best: visible, familiar, and easy to show working on a handful of examples. It is often the worst place to start, because the qualities that make it demonstrable — small volume, high visibility, unforgiving stakes — are exactly the qualities that make failure expensive.

The candidates worth pursuing tend to be duller: high volume, moderate stakes, rules that are real but tacit, and a checking step that someone quietly does well.
